## Context

An interaction function asks a person or a system through a channel and takes the answer later (0010). A webhook channel's receiver answers within the delivery, on a channel with `answers: true`, or later with the request's answer token; an MCP channel delivers the request as one call of one tool of a tool server, such as a tool that posts a message in a chat, and the answer can only come back through `answer_interaction`, over the API or through an agent the person talks to (0016, amendments, episode 7). On 2026-10-07 a person approved a draft twice in a chat the brain had posted it in, and nothing took the approval: the brain had posted through a gateway-style tool server that only calls out, which can receive nothing, and no part of the brain listens for a reply. The person wants to answer where the question reached them, "approve" or "reject, the second point is wrong", and have the brain take it. The owner's constraints: the person talks to brains; everything agent-facing is over MCP; no infrastructure the person cannot set up; a chat is the first case, but any chat-like tool server with a tool that sends and a tool that reads what came since, or a receiver the brain can post to, must fit one rule.

What the comparison decides, point by point:

1. **Every chat pushes, and every chat can be polled.** Slack, Teams and Discord deliver a reply to a public address or a socket the app keeps open, and each also answers a read of what came since a point: Slack by `oldest`, Discord by `after`, Teams by a `GET` of the chat. Make's own chat triggers poll on a schedule. A brain whose chat is behind a tool server that only calls out has no address the chat could reach and no socket, so today it reads; the hosted runtime, which has a public origin, will take pushes. Both must be one rule, or the second will be a second answering path. So this record defines the reading now and the push's shape now, and builds the reading first (§1, §9).
2. **A message is a conversation and an id, and a reply names what it answers.** Slack's `channel` and `ts` with `thread_ts`, Teams' `conversation.id` and activity `id` with `replyToId`, Discord's channel and `id` with `message_reference.message_id`: three vendors, one shape. The send answers with the identity, and Teams says in so many words that the sender must keep it. So the delivery keeps what the send answered, as two parts the channel's setting names, and a reply is matched to its request by the part it names (§2, §4, §6).
3. **Reading is rate-limited per method and per workspace, and the limits change under you.** Slack's Tier 3 is fifty a minute per method per workspace, and since May 2025 one a minute with fifteen objects for a class of apps; Teams allows one read a second on a given chat; every one of them answers a 429 with the wait. So the brain reads once per conversation, not per request, backs off, honours `Retry-After` as the next due time, and lets the operator set the floor for a server whose limit they know (§5, §12).
4. **Who may answer is named, and a stranger's click changes nothing.** n8n restricts approvers to listed users and tells anyone else privately "the workflow keeps waiting"; Zapier's reviewers are named accounts; GitHub's are the environment's reviewers with linked accounts; PagerDuty's and Opsgenie's are the responder the message reached. So a request names the party whose reply counts, `from`, the party it went `to` unless the function says otherwise, and a reply from anyone else is not an answer and not recorded (§3, §6).
5. **A closed question takes a fixed vocabulary, confirmed; an open one takes words.** PagerDuty's "Reply 4: Ack, 6: Resolve" and Opsgenie's "ack 4421", confirmed by text; Zapier's and n8n's buttons with a note or a free-text form. So a function's reply rule maps the first word of a reply to a value of its answer's enumeration, with the words that mean each, and the rest to a note, or the whole reply to a text, and a reply the rule cannot read is refused and the party told how to answer, through the same channel, at most a few times (§3, §6).
6. **The answer is durable and deduplicated by identity.** Temporal's signals are written to the workflow's history; Slack retries an event three times and names the attempt; n8n's second click meets a resumed workflow. So a reply is a fact on the request's stream with the reply's identity, a second taking of the same reply is refused by the stream's decider, and the run settles from the fact, so a stop between the two loses nothing (§6, §7, §8).
7. **A deadline bounds the wait everywhere.** Zapier's timeout then skip or end, n8n's Limit Wait Time, Pipedream's 24 hours, Temporal's timer, Camunda's due date. The request's expiry already does (0010), and it bounds the reading too: a conversation with no open request is read once more and then rested (§5).
8. **MCP's server-initiated messages do not reach a brain that opens a session per call.** They travel only on a stream the client opened and keeps open; the brain takes a session for a call and lets it go (`packages/mcp/src/connections/server-links.ts:120-125`), and a gateway that only calls out has no event to notify of in the first place. Elicitation and sampling are a server's requests of the client's user and model, the opposite direction of a person answering a brain. So nothing in the protocol replaces reading today; a tool server that comes to hold a conversation as a subscribable resource is named under what this record does not decide (§15).

## Decision

### 1. The rule: a reply is an answer the delivery brings back, taken by one path

A function that delivers through a tool may say, in its `replies` block, how the replies to what it sent are read (0010, amendment, a function owns its delivery). While a request that takes an answer stands open through such a delivery, the brain reads the replies of the conversation the request was delivered into, through the same tool server and the same call path a delivery uses, once per conversation and not once per request, on a cadence that backs off and stops when the conversation holds no open request. A reply counts only from the party the request names as its answerer, once per reply; its words are mapped to the answer by the request's reply rule and checked against the request's answer schema as any answer is; the answer is recorded on the request's stream with the reply's identity as its evidence, and the run settles from that fact by the path #125 gave an answer given within a delivery. A reply the rule cannot read is refused, recorded, and the party told how to answer, through the tool the function names in `tell`.

The one taking path is the rule this record adds to 0010's: a reply the brain reads is matched to its request by the identity the delivery kept, checked to be from the answerer, deduplicated by its identity, mapped by the reply rule, checked by `checkedAnswer`, and recorded as `reply_taken` or `reply_refused`; the settlement follows from the fact. The reads go through the delivery's call path, `callOnce`, and the due-work lanes; the answer goes through the one answering path, `answeredSettlement` under the settlement key. Nothing is scheduled by a timer of its own, called by a path of its own, or answered by a path of its own. A read that finds nothing new is not a fact, and the cadence of reading lives in the projection it schedules; §5 and §6 state that exception and its reasons.

### 2. The `replies` block of a function

A delivery through a tool may carry an optional block, `replies`, which says how a conversation is keyed, how its new replies are read, where in the read's answer each reply's sender, words, identity and parent are, and how the party is told when a reply was not an answer. Where the message a delivery sent is identified in the send's answer belongs to the delivery, `deliver.sent`, since the pointers read the send's answer: a function writes `deliver` and `replies` in its document, and nothing else holds them (0010, amendment, §1). A delivery without `replies` delivers as today and takes no reply; one with `sent` and without `replies` still records what it was delivered as.

```yaml
deliver:
  server: chat
  tool: post_message
  with:
    channel: '{{ to }}'
    text: '{{ message }}'
  sent:
    conversation: /channel
    id: /ts
replies:
  conversation: '{{ sent.conversation }}/{{ sent.id }}'
  tool: thread_replies
  with:
    channel: '{{ sent.conversation }}'
    ts: '{{ sent.id }}'
    oldest: '{{ since | default: "0" }}'
    limit: '15'
  read:
    list: /messages
    order: oldest_first
    each:
      id: /ts
      sender: /user
      text: /text
      to: /thread_ts
  wait: PT1M
  tell:
    with:
      channel: '{{ sent.conversation }}'
      thread_ts: '{{ sent.id }}'
      text: '{{ message }}'
```

| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

Liquid renders what the brain sends, over named fields, as a delivery's `with` does; pointers read what a tool answered. A template over a tool's answer would be the brain reading data as a program, and an answer is data and never instructions (0003 §3); a pointer names a place and nothing else, is checked for its shape at start, and is the form the API already uses to name a place in a value. The **document** a pointer reads is the tool's `structuredContent` when the tool gives one, otherwise its first text block parsed as JSON, else that text; a tool that gives neither has no document, and a pointer that finds nothing, in a text or in no document, fails as `unreadable`, the rule of `answerDocument` ([decision 0021](0021-asking-a-system.md), §1). The pointers' values must be text, or whole numbers, which are written as text: one chat's `ts` is a string, another chat's id a string.

The templates are compiled when the document is saved, each with the names it may read, and refused with the document's line, as a delivery's are, through the one `compiledRoute` of 0010's amendment §4 (`default` is among the filters the engine keeps, `packages/specs/src/template/template-engine.ts:34`); a pointer that is not a JSON Pointer, the empty pointer among them, in the one sentence every pointer of the format shares, "Expected a JSON Pointer such as /messages: it starts with a slash, writes ~ as ~0 and a slash within a name as ~1", a `read.order` that is neither value, a `wait` outside its bounds, and a `replies` block without `deliver.sent` or without `output.schema`, are refused with their line and never a value. A function's `tool` and `tell.tool` outside the server's `allowed` end its run at the start, `unavailable` `tool_not_offered`, as its `deliver.tool` does (0010, amendment, §2). A server's secrets never reach a template, a pointer or a record, as today: a template may not hold `${`.

### 3. The function document: `from` and `reply`

```markdown
---
description: Send the draft to its owner in the chat and take their approval
to: '{{ input.owner }}'
expires: P2D
deliver:
  server: chat
  tool: post_message
  with:
    channel: '{{ to }}'
    text: '{{ message }}'
  sent:
    conversation: /channel
    id: /ts
replies:
  conversation: '{{ sent.conversation }}/{{ sent.id }}'
  tool: thread_replies
  with:
    channel: '{{ sent.conversation }}'
    ts: '{{ sent.id }}'
    oldest: '{{ since | default: "0" }}'
    limit: '15'
  read:
    list: /messages
    order: oldest_first
    each: { id: /ts, sender: /user, text: /text, to: /thread_ts }
  wait: PT1M
  tell:
    with:
      channel: '{{ sent.conversation }}'
      thread_ts: '{{ sent.id }}'
      text: '{{ message }}'
input:
  schema:
    type: object
    required: [owner, draft]
    properties:
      owner: { type: string }
      draft: { type: string, maxLength: 4000 }
output:
  schema:
    type: object
    required: [choice]
    properties:
      choice: { type: string, enum: [approve, reject] }
      note: { type: string, maxLength: 2000 }
---

Here is the draft:

{{ input.draft }}

Reply **approve** or **reject**, with a note if you reject.
```

`from` is an optional template over `input`, `today` and `now`, like `to`, rendering the party whose reply counts, the **answerer**: `to` itself when left out, which is right when the request goes to a person, and a person's identity when it goes to a room. A request sent to a room must name who answers: a room is nobody's sender, so a request sent to one without `from` takes no reply, and "anyone in the room" stays out of this record (§15). It is rendered and bounded as `to` is, 256 bytes without a control character (`interactionBounds.toBytes`), and recorded in the request as `answerer`. Where a prompt needs a person's identity as a chat names it, `test_tool_call` on the tool that lists them is one test away (0018 §10).

`reply` is the **reply rule**: a map from a property of `output.schema` to where in a reply its value comes from. A value is `word`, `rest` or `text`, or an object with `from` and, for `word`, `words`:

- `word`: the first word of the reply, lowercased, with punctuation stripped from its ends, must be one of the property's `enum` values or one of the `words` the rule lists for a value; a property with `from: word` must be a string with an `enum`;
- `rest`: what follows the first word, trimmed; absent from the answer when nothing follows;
- `text`: the whole reply, trimmed.

Each named property must be a top-level string property of `output.schema`; each key of `words` must be one of the enum; a word may mean one value only; at most 16 values with at most 16 words each, each word at most 64 bytes; checked when the document is saved, with the line. When `reply` is left out, the rule is derived where it is plain: an answer schema that is an object whose required properties are exactly one string with an `enum` takes `{ <it>: word }`; one whose required properties are exactly one string without an `enum` takes `{ <it>: text }`; any other schema has no rule, so its requests take no reply and are answered through `answer_interaction` alone. The rule a request uses is the one its function had when it was asked: it is recorded in the deferral's record as `reply`, beside `answer_schema` and `answerer`, for the reason 0019 recorded the schema; and so are a function's `deliver` and `replies` blocks, as 0010's amendment §3 says.

### 4. The delivery keeps what the tool answered

`delivery_ended` gains two fields. **`delivered_as`**, `{ conversation, id }`, the two parts the delivery's `sent` pointers read from the document the send's tool answered, each at most 256 bytes of text without a control character, present whenever the delivery writes `sent` and both could be read, whether or not it reads replies, so the history says what the message was delivered as. **`replies_in`**, `{ server, tool, key }`, the server, the reading tool and the conversation key the `conversation` template rendered, present when the request takes an answer and its delivery reads replies: the projection of §5 keys its row from this one fact, by the tool; the server and the tool of the attempt are on `delivery_started` (0010, amendment, §3). When a pointer cannot be read, the attempt is still `delivered`, since the message went out, with `detail` saying which pointer could not be read, and neither field: such a request takes no reply and waits for `answer_interaction`, and its listing says so (§10). A delivery without `sent` reads no pointer and delivers as today.

For that, the mcp package's `callOnce` answers the call whole, as 0010's amendment §3 shapes it: the outcome, the fields `callAnswered` builds under the server's `Recording`, the result as `content` and `structuredContent` scrubbed of the server's secrets, the failure's words and `Retry-After`, in place of its JSON cut at 4 KiB; `deliveryBounds.resultBytes` goes, since no caller cut by it meant anything; and the delivery records its own `delivery_started` before the call is sent, with the fields `callStarted` builds less `call_id`, which `startOf` gives (0010, amendment, §3). `callOnce` takes its metadata from the caller, `meta` on the call as `CallerContext` carries it since 0018 (`caller-context.ts:5-11`): a delivery gives `{ execution_id, delivery_id }` as today, a read and a telling give `{ "com.beonauto/conversation_call_id": <id> }`, and `DeliveryCall` loses `executionId` and `deliveryId`.

### 5. Conversations are the schedule of reading, kept as a projection its reader advances

What is read is the **conversations projection**, `conversations`, one row per brain, server, reading tool and conversation key, the row's key `server/tool/key`, since neither a server's name nor a tool's holds a slash (`packages/mcp/src/names/tool-reference.ts:8-10`): two functions that read one thread with one tool share one read.

| Column         | Holds                                                                                        | Written by                                          |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| `server`       | The tool server                                                                              | The fold                                            |
| `tool`         | The tool that reads                                                                          | The fold                                            |
| `conversation` | The key                                                                                      | The fold                                            |
| `since`        | The cursor: the identity of the newest reply the last recorded read considered, or none      | The fold, from `replies_read`                       |
| `last_fact`    | The id of the fact that last changed the row, the cause of the next read's fact              | The fold                                            |
| `joined_by`    | The id of the delivery that last joined the row, against which the reader's advance compares | The fold on a join                                  |
| `open`         | Whether a request may still be answered in it, as far as the brain last knew                 | The fold on a join; the reader                      |
| `active_at`    | When a request last joined, or a reply from an answerer was last found                       | The fold on a join; the reader                      |
| `reads`        | The reads since `active_at`, which set the cadence                                           | The fold on a join; the reader                      |
| `next_read_at` | When the next read is due, or none while the conversation rests                              | The fold on a join; the reader                      |
| `due_at`       | `next_read_at` while `open`, which the partial index across brains serves                    | Derived from the two by the mapping, on every write |

Two facts make and move its rows:

- `delivery_ended` with `replies_in`, on a run's stream: the row for the key is made or woken, `open` true, `reads` 0, `active_at` the fact's time, `next_read_at` the earlier of what it was and the fact's time plus the first wait, `last_fact` the fact's id;
- `replies_read`, on the call's own stream (§6): `since` the cursor the fact carries, `last_fact` its id.

Everything else on the row is the **cadence**, and the reader keeps it: before a call, `next_read_at` a minute ahead, the bound of a read in flight (`attemptInFlightMs`, one constant for the attempts and the reads), so the row leaves the page while the read is in flight; after every read, recorded or not, `reads` one more, or 0 with `active_at` now when a reply from an answerer was found, and `next_read_at` now plus the wait of the cadence, or plus the `Retry-After` a 429 carried, at most an hour; and when the conversation holds no open request, `open` false and `next_read_at` none, until a delivery wakes the row. For that the ledger's projections gain one write beside the fold, `advanceRow(projection, brain, key, columns)`: it writes, for one row and in a transaction of its own, the columns a projection declares its reader advances and no other; the fold's write of a row leaves those columns as they stand unless its mapping sets them, which it does on a join alone, so a fold that lands beside an advance cannot undo it; and the advance is a compare-and-set on `joined_by`, the id of the delivery that last joined the row, which the reader passes from the row it read, so an advance that lands after a join changes nothing and the join's cadence stands; and the fill never writes them, so a rebuilt row holds what the facts say and what the fold set at the last join, `open` true and a `next_read_at` that has passed. **A rebuild restarts every conversation's cadence from "read now"**: each conversation that ever had a join is read once from its cursor, and the ones with nothing open rest again at that read, without a call. Nothing a reply needs lives in the cadence: the cursor is the fact's, and a reply is taken once by its identity on the request's stream (§7), so a rebuild can neither lose a reply nor take one twice; it costs one read of the open-requests projection per conversation.

This is the one state the brain keeps that no fact stands for, and the exception to the terminology page's "a defined derivation of a view or state from event history", decided with §6's: the row is the schedule of reads, and a read that found nothing is not a fact. The open-requests projection, whose schedule is wholly folded from facts, stays as it is: every attempt and every answer is a fact there.

So the ledger's projections generalise from projections of runs to **projections keyed by what their mapping says**: a projection names the stream kinds it folds, `executions` for every projection that exists today and `executions` and `conversation-calls` for this one, and `keyOf(event, stream)`, the key of the row an event changes, the stream's id for a projection of runs and, here, the server, the tool and the key that `replies_in` or `replies_read` carries; the table's key columns are `brain_key` and `row_key`; the fill replays every stream of the named kinds; `ProjectedRunRow` becomes `ProjectedKeyedRow` with `key`; and a projection may declare the columns its reader advances. The two projections of today, `run_outcomes` and `open_requests`, declare `executions`, the default key and no advanced column, and their tables take the next version, which the first start fills as 0019's did, dropping the earlier tables; nothing is live, nothing keeps an old name, and a development store with a table a version cannot read is deleted.

The open-requests projection gains what the reading needs on each row, beside the `delivery` and `replies` of 0010's amendment §3: `conversation`, `sent_conversation` and `sent_id`, from `replies_in` and `delivered_as`, null until a delivery kept them; `answerer` and `reply`, the rule as JSON text, from the deferral's record, kept as 0019 keeps `answer_schema`; `reply_refusals` and `refusals_told`, counted from `reply_refused`; and the index `by_conversation` on `open`, `conversation` and `requested_at`. A row stands `answered` on `reply_taken`. The reading a row works with is `routeOf` (0010, amendment, §4): the `replies` block its row records.

The **cadence**, `replyReadWaits`, from `active_at`:

| Read since the conversation was last active | Wait before it                      |
| ------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| 1st                                         | 5 s                                 |
| 2nd, 3rd, 4th                               | 10 s, 20 s, 40 s                    |
| 5th to 18th                                 | 60 s each, to about fifteen minutes |
| 19th and after                              | 300 s                               |

The reading's `wait`, the oldest kept request's, is the floor of every wait; a 429 the read met raises the next wait to the `Retry-After` it carries, at most an hour; a read that failed otherwise waits as the cadence says. A request answered within a minute costs at most four reads; within a quarter of an hour, eighteen, the last at 915 s; a conversation open a whole day, 302; one open thirty days, 8,654. Against the limits of §Context: a chat that allows fifty reads a minute per method and workspace carries, at the floor, four conversations in their first minute or 250 at rest, and past that its 429s set the pace; where a tool server allows one read a minute for every conversation of a workspace, the brain's reads meet a 429 at their second read, each is recorded as a failed read, and they take turns a minute apart as the server asks, until the function's author sets `wait: PT1M`, as the example does. The default stays `PT5S`, since the five-second first read is what a person answering "approve" feels, and a 429 costs a recorded read and nothing else.

The conversations are a second `DueWork` beside the open requests (`conversationsDue`), performed by the loop in lanes of its own, so a burst of reads after a restart never holds a delivery or an expiry, which stay in the open requests' lanes: each due row is one item keyed by brain, server, tool and key, `callsOut` true, performed in the outbound lane, 256 in flight and 16 at once (`dueInOneTick`, `duePerformedAtOnce`). The page the loop reads for it has room for the held and the in-flight rows as the open requests' does since #125 (`due-work.ts:137`), an in-flight read leaves its page by the reader's advance before the call, and an expiry can never wait behind a read, since the two works read two pages. A read cannot be starved by a delivery nor starve one: the lanes are the work's own; a read waits only on reads, and sixteen reads that hang at their 40 s bound hold the lane's permits for 40 s while the rest of the 256 wait in memory, filtered from the next page by their key (`due-work.ts:119`). The loop performs due work on the lease holder alone (0010 §2), so two hosts never read one conversation at once; across a handover, a read the old holder still had in flight may land its fact beside the new holder's, and the replies both saw are taken once, by their identity.

### 6. A read, step by step

The perform of a due conversation:

1. Advances the row, `next_read_at` a minute ahead, so a read lost to a stop is read again a minute later and never twice at once.
2. Reads the open requests of the conversation from the open-requests projection, `open` and `conversation` equal, oldest first, at most 100 (`by_conversation`), and keeps those that stand `delivered` or `retrying`: a row that stands `answered` or `cancelling` takes no reply. When none is kept, the row rests, `open` false and `next_read_at` none, with no call and no fact; a delivery wakes it.
3. Takes the reading of the oldest kept request through `routeOf`, the `replies` block its row records; renders `replies.with` over that request's `to` and `sent`, the key, and `since`, the row's cursor or an empty text, and calls `replies.tool` through `callOnce` with the delivery's server, under `namedLinks` as the delivery was, within its bounds, 10 s to open and to list, and 30 s to answer, scrubbed, as a delivery is; a tool the server's `allowed` no longer names is a failed read, `tool_not_offered`, recorded in step 7.
4. Reads the document, the list at `read.list`, and considers every reply in it, in the tool's order, which bounds the document the read takes at 64 KiB as JSON (`toolBounds.resultBytes`); a document that cannot be read, a list that is not an array, or a result past the bound ends the read `unreadable` or `too_large`, a failed read recorded in step 7, which whoever reads the history fixes in the function's `replies` or the tool's `limit`.
5. For each reply, in order: its `id`, `sender`, `text` and `to` by the pointers; a reply without an `id` or a `sender` is passed over. It belongs to the kept request whose `delivered_as.id` equals its `to`; a reply with no `to`, or on a reading without the pointer, belongs to the conversation's one kept request, and when several are kept it is **ambiguous**: recorded as refused on the newest of them, since that is the request a guess would have answered and its `tell` is the one that speaks, and never taken, because a guess that silently answers the wrong question is worse than a refusal with words. A reply that belongs to none, or whose `sender` is not the request's answerer, is nothing and leaves no trace: a stranger's words are never recorded in a reply fact, though where the server's entry records content the read's `result_json` keeps the whole answer the tool gave, as §12 allows. A reply from the answerer whose `id` the request's stream already holds is nothing too: the decider refuses it (§7), as it refuses a second reply to a request this read already answered. Otherwise its `text`, bounded at 8 KiB (`interactionBounds.messageBytes`, a longer one refused as `too_long`), is mapped by the request's recorded reply rule to an answer and checked by `checkedAnswer` against the recorded `answer_schema`, the one answering check:
   - an answer that fits: `reply_taken` on the request's stream, `{ server, tool, reply: { id, sender }, answer, at }`, with the server and the tool that read it, caused by the row's `last_fact`, and then the settlement of §7, which the due work also makes from the fact should the server stop between the two;
   - a first word the rule has no value for, or no words at all, as a reply that is an attachment alone: `reply_refused` with `because` `not_an_answer`; an answer that fails the schema: `reply_refused` with `because` `invalid` and the issues under `/answer`; a reply past its bound: `too_long`; a reply that names no request where several are open: `ambiguous`; each `{ server, tool, reply: { id, sender }, because, issues?, told }`, caused by the row's `last_fact`, and at most ten per request, past which such a reply leaves no trace.
6. Tells the party, when the refused request's reading has a `tell` block and the request has had fewer than three refusals told: the words are the function's own, "To answer, reply with one of approve, reject; what follows is kept as the note." built from the rule's values and words, or the issues in words for an answer that did not fit, "A reply may hold at most 8,192 bytes." for one too long, or "Several questions are open here; reply to the message of the one you answer." for one that is ambiguous; each telling is a call of the brain's own, `telling_started` before it is sent and `telling_ended` after, on a stream of its own, caused by the `reply_refused` that `told` true, made through `callOnce` with `tell.tool` and `tell.with` rendered over the refused request's `to` and `sent` and `message`. A telling that fails is recorded and not tried again; the question's own message said how to answer.
7. Records the read when it is a fact, and advances the row. The read is a fact when it returned at least one reply, or failed: one event, `replies_read`, on the stream `conversation-calls/<id>` under the brain, a UUID of its own, carrying the fields `callStarted` and `callAnswered` build, the server, the tool, the arguments' size and digest, the outcome, `result`, `tool_error`, `server_failure`, `timed_out`, `unreadable`, `too_large` or `tool_not_offered`, the result's size and digest, the duration, the JSON-RPC id, the server's request id where the entry names one, and with `record_content` the arguments and the result, scrubbed and cut to 4 KiB as stored; and the reading's own fields, `conversation`, `since`, the identity of the newest considered reply, the last in `oldest_first` order and the first in `newest_first`, or the cursor it had, `replies` considered, `taken`, `refused`, `retry_after_ms` when a 429 said so, `by` `brain:<brain>` and `at`; caused by the row's `last_fact`. Then, in one advance, the cadence of §5: `reads`, `active_at`, `next_read_at` and `open`, whether any kept request remains unanswered.

**The exception, stated.** Every call the brain makes is recorded, and its start before it is sent (0003 §5, rule 2; 0018 §4). A read that finds nothing new is recorded nowhere, for three reasons. It changes nothing outside the brain, so a read the brain cannot account for is a read made again, where a lost write could be a change made twice. A conversation nobody answers would otherwise write two facts every five minutes, 604 a day and 17,300 a month for one open request, onto a ledger that keeps every fact for good, and most reads find nothing. And a start recorded before the call cannot know whether the call will be a fact, so a start without an end would read as an outcome unknown for every quiet read; the read that is a fact is therefore one fact, appended after the call, carrying what a start and an answer carry. The telling keeps the rule whole, since it writes. What the exception costs: a read that a stop interrupts leaves no trace and is made again a minute later; the operator reads in the history every read that found something or failed, and in `list_interactions` the conversation and the refusals of each request; and the cadence lives in the projection, as §5 says.

**Nothing is lost and nothing is taken twice.** The cursor moves only by a `replies_read` fact, appended after every reply up to it was taken, refused or found to be nobody's, so a reply the cursor has passed was handled; a reply the cursor has not passed is read again. Each `reply_taken` and `reply_refused` is on the request's stream before the cursor moves, so a read made again after a stop or a lease handover meets its own earlier takings and the decider refuses them (§7). A ledger that fails during the taking fails the perform: the loop holds the row and tries again, nothing advanced and nothing lost, and the replies taken before the failure are refused by identity on the retry. A reply to a request that has ended, expired, cancelled or answered through `answer_interaction`, belongs to no kept request and is nothing; the question's message said until when it stands. A second reply to one question, in the same read or a later one, meets a request that stands `answered` and is nothing. A reply quoting the question is words to the rule like any other, unless the tool names the quoted message at the `to` pointer. An edited reply the tool lists under its id is one the stream already holds; a reply deleted after it was taken leaves the answer standing (§15).

A reply is matched and taken in the brain's own code, never by a model: the words are the rule's, and a reply the rule cannot read is refused, not interpreted. The cursor is the tool's own ordering, so the brain compares no identities; a tool that lists newest first gives its first item as the cursor, with the limit §2 states.

### 7. The answer settles the run by the one answering path

`reply_taken` makes the row stand `answered`, and the row is due at once; the settlement is `answeredSettlement(by, answer, at)` (`request-endings.ts:15-18`), the actor `brain:<brain>`, the caller a run of the brain acts as (0010, amendments, an expiry is settled as the brain), under the settlement key of the answer's digest, so the same words again settle what landed and a second reply with other words meets a settled run and is `conflict`, which the read takes as the no-op it is. The record of the settlement is `{ answered_by, answered_at, reply: { id, sender } }`: the reply's identity is the evidence, and **`claimed_for` is not written**, since `claimed_for` is what a caller claims and is never checked (0010 §2), while the sender of a reply was checked against the answerer and recorded as what it is. The delivery that read the reply is on the request's facts. A reply read after `expires_at`, before the expiry is recorded, still answers, as an answer through `answer_interaction` does, and the expiry wins once it is recorded.

The `settledFromDelivery`, `lastDelivery` and `cancelledRequest` of #125 (`request-ledger.ts:77-89`, `execution-state.ts:35-36`, `request-cancels.ts:6-18`) become the rule for every answer a reply brings back, under route-free names: the run's state keeps `broughtAnswer`, `{ answer, at, cause }`, set by a `reply_taken` alone; the due work settles a row that stands `answered` from it (`settledFromBroughtAnswer`); a cancel finds it first and settles with it rather than as cancelled (`CancelledRun`, `primitive.ts:66-71`). `answer_interaction` on a request that stands `answered` stays `conflict` with its words: the answer the reply brought back came first. A cancel asked before the reply is read wins: the decider refuses `reply_taken` and `reply_refused` while the run has a cancel asked, as it refuses a new delivery (`work-decisions.ts:50-52`).

The two reply facts are an execution command of their own, `reply`, decided by `decideReply` beside `decideOutboundCall`: refused once the run has ended, once a cancel is asked, for a `reply_taken` once the run holds a `broughtAnswer`, for a `reply_refused` once the run holds ten, and when the reply's `id` is one the run's stream already holds in `reply_taken` or `reply_refused`, which the state keeps as `repliesSeen`, at most eleven; recorded through `replyRecorder(ledger)`, the shape of `outboundCallRecorder` (`run-work/outbound-calls.ts:34-47`). The two types join the brain's reserved types (`reserved-attributes.ts:34-59`), as `replies_read`, `telling_started` and `telling_ended` do.

### 8. After a restart, nothing is lost

The schedule is the projection and the cursor is on its row, so a server that starts finds every conversation whose `next_read_at` has passed and reads it from `since`: what arrived meanwhile is read, in the lanes' bounds, 16 at once and 256 in flight, and under the tool server's 429s. A read whose row was advanced and whose fact or advance never came is read again a minute after it began. A `reply_taken` whose settlement the stopped server never made is settled by the due work from the fact. A reply taken before the stop is on the request's stream, so reading it again is refused by the decider and tells nobody twice. A telling started and not ended is not made again. On a store a later version opens, the conversation's row is rebuilt from the facts as every projection's is (`packages/ledger/README.md:257`), with its cadence restarted as §5 says.

### 9. The push form, not decided

A reply posted to the brain, rather than read by it, is not decided: it waits for a tool server that offers a conversation as a subscribable resource, which 0010's amendment §9 lists among what it does not decide. What a chat's own push needs, a relay per tool holding the chat's signature, its subscription challenge and its retries, was surveyed while this record was drafted; the brain reads wherever a tool server can be asked, and whatever form a push takes later, it shares every step of §6 from the fifth on.

## Consequences

- A person answers where the question reached them, in words, and the brain takes it within seconds at first and within five minutes at most while the request stands, with no agent in the loop and nothing the person set up; an agent that is told "approve" meanwhile answers the same request and finds it answered.
- One rule, one path: the delivery keeps the message's identity, the read goes through the delivery's call path and the loop's lanes, the reply is taken by the one answering check and settled by the one settlement, no timer, no second scheduler, no second call path, no second answering path.
- A function that delivers through a tool reads its replies from the `replies` block its request recorded, and the reading knows the server, the tool and the conversation: an agent writes the whole of it from what the tools answered, and nothing is set up for it (0010, amendment).
- The cost is stated: reading is calls, bounded by the cadence, the lanes and the expiry, at most 8,654 reads for a request open a month and a few for one answered within minutes; a quiet conversation writes no fact, a read that found something or failed writes one, on a stream of its own that reaches no recall function; a tool server's limits are met by `wait` and `Retry-After`.
- Two exceptions, each stated with its reason: a read that finds nothing is not recorded (§6), and the cadence of reading is state in a projection that no fact stands for, restarted from "read now" by a rebuild (§5).
- The ledger's projections become general: a row keyed by what its mapping says, over the stream kinds it names, with columns its reader may advance, which the next schedule the brain keeps will use as this one does.
- Debts paid: the delivery keeps what the tool answered; `callOnce` takes its metadata from its caller and cuts nothing; the answer-within-delivery path of #125 becomes the rule for every answer a reply brings back; the brain's own calls have one journal; a request names its answerer.
- The cost in code: the interaction package grows by the reply rule, the reading and the conversations projection, which its folders hold within their bounds; the mcp package by the result carried whole and the one journal; the ledger by the keyed projection and the reader's advance; the specs package by two facts and a command.
